    Notebooks you keep and share¶

    In brief. Every investigation is a notebook, laid out so the answer comes first: the question, what the data shows and what it means, and the proposed actions; then the findings, each a sentence with the table, figure or log view it rests on; then how it was done. Your marks are there when it took up one of your questions. Notebooks are saved in the workspace and listed on the Research page with who wrote them and their finding. A notebook refers to flights by their fingerprint, never by file, so it travels: export it, and another owner imports it, reads your investigation, and with EIS on their computer runs it on their flights.

    What a notebook holds¶

    A notebook has seven sections, always in this order. The first three are what to read when you have five minutes:

    1. The question — as asked, and its scope: which flights were looked at, which manual pages were used, what was taken as given, what the logs can't tell.
    2. Answer — What the data shows, three to five sentences, each with its count (in 93 of 98 takeoffs); and What it means — a hypothesis, how sure Claude is, and what would change its mind.
    3. Proposed actions — numbered, each saying what to do, who would do it (you, your mechanic, a manufacturer), what it would settle, and when EIS can watch it, re-check after the next N flights. Or None, and why.
    4. Findings — each one a sentence and the evidence it rests on: a table, a figure or a log view (the Flight view's chart for a window, with Open flight), numbered Table 1, Figure 2, Log view 3, with a caption saying what it shows. The calculation behind it is folded under show the calculation.
    5. How this was done — the plan, the data and how flights were cut into slices, the method, the alternatives ruled out, and anything from outside your logs labelled as context.
    6. Follow-ups — dated, when Claude came back to the notebook; each ends with what changed above.
    7. Working — the exploring Claude did on the way, kept but folded.

    Sections 1–3 are written when Claude finishes, from what it states as the answer — not left as prose somewhere in the middle. The notebook also holds the files a cell wrote (a CSV, a figure) and a header: who wrote it, its status, how many actions it proposes, the engine version, and whether its outputs are current for this workspace. Notebooks written before 1.1.2 have no sections; they read as they always did.

    It does not hold your logs. A log view draws its window from the flight in your workspace; where that flight is absent, it shows the picture saved with the notebook.

    It is an ordinary Jupyter .ipynb: it opens in JupyterLab or VS Code, and runs there against your workspace (Run a notebook in JupyterLab).

    Keeping¶

    Notebooks live in the workspace and travel with a workspace export. On the Research page, Re-run (with EIS on your computer) runs every code cell again on your current flights; outputs that changed wait for you to Accept or Discard, cell by cell or all at once. That is the cheap way to ask the same question next season. Delete… removes a notebook and its files.

    Following up¶

    The conversation is where you and Claude think out loud; the notebook is what stays. Analysis Claude relies on goes into the notebook as a cell; exploring goes under Working; a quick look to decide where to go next stays in the chat. A cell that fails is fixed in place, not left behind a working copy, and Claude cannot finish while one is still failing. When you talk on after a notebook is finished and the answer changes — a new measurement, an alternative ruled out — Claude adds a dated follow-up under Follow-ups, ending with what changed, and finishes the notebook again. While it works the notebook reads reopened; afterwards the new Answer is on top and the earlier one is folded inside it, with its date. Say "put that in the notebook" whenever you want something kept. Facts about the aircraft — a part changed, a setting — belong in your notes in Settings → Aircraft; Claude asks you to add them there.

    Sharing¶

    Two exports, on the notebook's page:

    • Export notebook (.zip) — the notebook and its files. Flights are named by fingerprint — a code derived from each log, meaningless to anyone who does not hold the same log.
    • Export as a page (.html) — one self-contained page anyone can open in a browser, with no EIS at all: the narrative, outputs, figures and log-view pictures. The one to send a mechanic or a manufacturer.

    Someone who receives the .zip (or a bare .ipynb) chooses Import a notebook… on their Research page. EIS matches its flights against their workspace: cells whose flights are here are live; cells whose flights are absent show the sender's outputs, read-only, and say so.

    Run this on my flights¶

    An imported notebook has one more button: Run this on my flights. It copies the notebook as a new one in your workspace — … — my flights — and, with EIS on your computer, runs every code cell against your own flights and keeps the outputs. Now the same measurements are about your engine, beside the sender's.

    This is how a finding spreads without anyone's logs moving: here is the notebook that found the oscillation on my aircraft — run it on yours. The receiving owner's data never leaves their machine, and the sender's never had to.

    What to do with this¶

    Keep a notebook even when it says something dull; re-running it next season is free. Before sending one, export it as a page and read it as the recipient will. When you receive one, run it on your flights before reading the sender's conclusions too closely.

    How it works¶

    Notebooks live in the workspace's studies/<id>/ folder (notebook.ipynb and files/) and are carried by workspace export. Their EIS metadata — author, status, question, summary, the inbox item or question it answered — sits in the notebook's own metadata (The notebook format). Import always creates a new notebook. Notebooks made before Research became notebook-only open as notebooks, converted on first load.
